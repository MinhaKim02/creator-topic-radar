# Research rubric

## Five independent assessments

| Dimension | Suggested labels | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Audience Interest | Strong observed / Limited observed / Early / Unassessed | Strength of accessible audience signals, not predicted demand |
| Freshness | New event / Old event + new development / Ongoing / Commentary only / Unclear | Timing of the story, not the age of a page |
| Factual Reliability | Supported / Qualified / Conflicting / Insufficient | Whether the core premise is supportable in its proposed wording |
| Duplicate Risk | Low / Medium / High / Unassessed | Semantic overlap with the supplied comparison set |
| Short-form Suitability | Strong / Needs narrowing / Weak | Ability to explain the question accurately in roughly 60 seconds |

Explain every label. Translate labels into the user's language as needed. Do not combine them into a single ranking score. Unknown and unassessed are not negative evidence.

## Candidate-level factual reliability

Judge the proposed core premise, not the amount of surrounding source material.

- **Supported:** Appropriate evidence establishes the core premise in its stated scope, with no unresolved material contradiction. “The maker announced X” may be supported while X itself remains a company claim.
- **Qualified:** The premise is usable only with explicit limits or attribution, and those qualifications still answer the question. Do not use this label to hide an unsupported central result.
- **Conflicting:** Relevant sources materially disagree about the core premise and the disagreement is unresolved. A nonessential peripheral discrepancy need not make the entire topic conflicting; disclose it and leave it out of the premise.
- **Insufficient:** The core premise lacks evidence, crucial sources are inaccessible, or essential scope/timing cannot be established.

Separate this candidate-level judgment from each statement's epistemic status. For example, a parts-ordering announcement can be supported as an announcement while the availability assertion is a company claim; a claim of 50% lower repair costs is insufficient without cost evidence.

## Verify the premise

Write the proposed central factual premise first. Locate evidence that supports that exact premise, not merely the entity or general subject.

Prefer direct evidence suited to the claim: official product/company material for announced capabilities, regulators and government records for decisions, filings/court records/datasets for legal or quantitative claims, papers and research institutions for scientific findings, and high-quality reporting for independently investigated facts. This is a contextual preference, not a universal credibility ladder. An official marketing statement supports what a company claims; it does not independently establish the promised outcome. Court allegations are not court findings. A paper's publication is not proof that every interpretation is settled.

Do not use creator commentary as final factual evidence. Trace its claims to original sources where possible. If direct sources are inaccessible, identify the reporting used, its limitations, and the resulting claim status.

| Status | Use when |
|---|---|
| Verified fact | Accessible appropriate evidence supports the precise wording and scope |
| Company claim | The company asserts a capability, result, or forecast not independently established |
| Reported claim | Reporting attributes the claim, but underlying evidence is not directly established |
| Analysis | A reasoned conclusion from identified facts; state the assumptions |
| Interpretation | A possible meaning or framing of evidence; attribute or explain it |
| Prediction | A future outcome that remains uncertain |
| Unknown | Evidence is absent, inaccessible, or unresolved |

Attach source title, URL/citation, publication date when known, and the exact proposition supported. Do not quote or reproduce full protected transcripts or articles. Verify central dates, quantities, currencies, measurement periods, units, geography, versions, and eligibility restrictions. Separate announced, demonstrated, launched, and broadly available functionality.

For conflicting numbers, compare definitions, periods, regions, gross/net measures, revisions, and timestamps. Explain a resolved difference. If unresolved, show both relevant figures and the uncertainty. Exclude an irreducibly ambiguous number from the proposed central explanation, or mark the topic provisional if that number is essential.

## Compare semantic duplicates

Inspect actual past descriptions or summaries when available. Titles alone may be insufficient. Compare:

- Entity and underlying event.
- Core question and causal mechanism.
- Argument, conclusion, and viewer takeaway.

Different entities can yield the same video if their question, mechanism, and takeaway repeat. The same entity can yield a distinct topic after a genuinely different event or question. A recent update is not automatically enough: explain what it changes for the viewer.

Compare candidates with one another before finalizing the shortlist. Merge different titles that explain the same event, mechanism, and takeaway. Keep two angles only if their central questions and explanations have distinct value for this brief; explain that distinction. This within-shortlist check is possible even when past-content history is absent, but does not establish low duplicate risk against unknown history.

Use **High** for substantially repeated question/mechanism/takeaway; **Medium** for meaningful overlap with a plausible distinct angle; **Low** for a demonstrably different story within the supplied set. Name the closest match and explain the overlap. If history is absent or thin, use **Unassessed**, or qualify that only the supplied titles were compared. Never claim channel-wide clearance from a partial archive.

## Assess short-form fit

Ask whether a viewer can follow one central question, one concrete change or tension, a short causal explanation, and a clear destination. Identify necessary prior knowledge and caveats. Consider concrete subjects, actions, or source material that make the explanation understandable; do not create a shot list here.

Use **Needs narrowing** when one evidenced sub-question fits but the full story does not. Use **Weak** when too much background or too many essential caveats would be required. A strong research topic can be a weak short-form topic. Do not make it fit by overstating causality or certainty.

## Check record match and audience relevance

Label a database/claim match **Direct**, **Partial**, **None**, or **Unassessed**, explaining what it supports and what is missing. Internal engagement does not establish truth or external creator interest.

Tie audience relevance to the brief: a practical consequence, changed choice, understandable conflict, or supported business mechanism. A company name does not require an investment angle. Do not assert financial outcomes merely because a product appears useful.

## Make a recommendation

Use **Recommend**, **Provisional**, or **Do not recommend**, with a reason. State research gaps and what would resolve them. Readiness reflects the evidence and brief, not predicted video performance. Keep early-interest candidates distinguishable from candidates with a supported current premise but no measured interest data.

Only Recommend entries belong in the recommended shortlist. Provisional leads may appear separately for follow-up; they do not count toward the recommended total. Treat the requested count as a maximum for all surfaced entries unless the user explicitly asks for more. Do not recommend an unsupported central premise simply because it has strong creator attention. Conversely, missing audience metrics need not prevent recommending a well-supported topic if relevance and short-form fit are clear and the interest gap is stated.
